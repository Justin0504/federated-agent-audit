\title{Agents Over-Share:\\
Center-Blind Auditing for Multi-Tenant Agent Privacy}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Author(s)}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Submitted for double-blind review}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Autonomous agents increasingly delegate work to one another across organizational
boundaries, and protocols such as A2A now standardize how they do so---while
carrying no notion of whom a datum concerns or who may receive it. We begin by
measuring what crosses. Across ten models and $2{,}880$ realistic hand-offs,
agents put a subject's regulated identifiers into messages bound for an outside
company in $96\%$ of them, and \emph{instructing them buys redaction rather than
privacy}. Handed the record's own policy---owner, permitted recipient, and the
fact that this recipient is not permitted---the frontier model stops pasting
identifiers almost perfectly ($44\%\!\to\!1\%$) and barely changes what it says
about the person ($40\%\!\to\!36\%$); pooled across all ten, $74\%$ of hand-offs
still leak. Models learn not to paste the account number and go on writing the
diagnosis.
What improves with capability is redaction, not disclosure; exposure does not decay
along a relay chain; and compositional inference \emph{rises} with capability. Privacy here is a
property of what the recipient can \emph{infer}, not of which tokens a message
contains---and every token-level defense we test, from the agent's own redaction to
Presidio and Llama Guard, fails in the same way. The safeguard has to be built at
the level of inference.

Building one is hard because no party may read the evidence. Classical
information-flow control assumes a trusted runtime that inspects every value it
labels; between mutually-distrusting organizations that party does not exist, and
its absence---not the lattice---is what makes this setting new. We present a
center-blind auditor that decides from desensitized metadata it cannot invert to
content, with a privacy-typing extension to A2A that makes the leak expressible at
all. We prove the center learns $O(\log)$ bits per message and measure it at a flat
$418$ bytes across a $1024\times$ range of message size; against an adversary that
knows the detector, $20{,}000$ randomized evasion strategies yield none that learns
more than the policy already permits. We report where it fails as carefully as
where it works: scored against ground truth we do not control, our own recall is
$0.75$, so the rates above are lower bounds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Agents are starting to talk to \emph{each other}. Protocols such as A2A (v1.0,
Linux Foundation), MCP, and ACP let agents built by different teams discover
capabilities and delegate work across organizational boundaries. As this fabric
forms, a question with no answer today becomes urgent: \emph{when my agent hands
data to your agent, what did it just share, and was it allowed to?}

This is already a live privacy problem, not a hypothetical one. Production agents
in regulated settings---healthcare schedulers, financial assistants, HR
copilots---increasingly delegate to third-party agents, and a single over-shared
identifier (an SSN, a diagnosis, an account number) crossing that organizational
boundary is a concrete privacy harm under regimes such as HIPAA and GDPR. It is
also the common case rather than the exception: across ten models and $2{,}880$ realistic hand-offs, agents put the subject's regulated identifiers into
a message bound for an outside company in $96\%$ of them. Our aim is a
deployable safeguard for exactly these interactions---one that runs today and sees
no raw content.

Three things make this hard at once. The leakage is where it is least watched:
inter-agent messages leak far more than final outputs, yet no current framework
monitors the internal channel~\cite{agentleak}. It is also compositional, arising
from messages that are each individually benign even when every agent obeys its
own policy~\cite{sumleaks}. And across organizations the parties do not trust one
another: the other agent is the adversary, and no one will route raw content
through a central observer, which is precisely what centralized observability
(LangSmith, Langfuse) requires. This last point is not merely an engineering
inconvenience. Decades of information-flow control rest on a trusted runtime that
may inspect every value it labels; between mutually-distrusting organizations
that party does not exist. The question this paper answers is what remains
auditable once it is taken away.

The protocols do not help. We verified against the A2A v1.0 specification that its
data model (\texttt{AgentCard}, \texttt{Task}, \texttt{Message}, \texttt{Part})
has \textbf{no data-governance semantics}: no field marks whom a datum is about,
who owns it, what it may be used for, or which agent may receive it. A2A provides
free-form \texttt{metadata}, an \texttt{extensions} array, and a loosely-specified
\texttt{tenant} field that routes but does not govern.

We close this gap. One idea runs through everything that follows: privacy is a
property of what the recipient can \emph{infer}, not of which tokens a message
contains, so both the threat and the defense have to be understood at that level.
Agents that redact still disclose; scanners that match still miss; the one
component that works accumulates evidence across messages rather than inspecting
any one of them. Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Instructions buy redaction, not privacy.} Hand the model the
record's own policy---owner, permitted recipient, purpose, and the fact that this
recipient is \emph{not} permitted---and $74\%$ of hand-offs still leak (ten models, $2{,}880$ trials). The frontier model shows why in its sharpest form: told
not to include identifiers, it complies almost perfectly
($44\%\!\to\!\mathbf{1\%}$), and barely changes what it discloses \emph{about the
person} ($40\%\!\to\!36\%$). ``Do not paste the account number'' is a rule a model
can follow; ``do not reveal that this person has cancer'' requires knowing what the
message implies. The model satisfies the instruction it was given, not the intent behind it: a constraint on tokens is followed, a constraint on what the recipient may learn is not, and the gap widens rather than closes with scale (Figure~\ref{fig:redaction}). Three other escapes close with it: the permissive phrasing in our
prompt is not the cause (removing it changes $96\%$ to $98\%$), capability buys better
redaction and no less disclosure, and exposure does not decay along a relay chain.
\item \textbf{Auditing when no party may read the content.} The premise classical
information-flow control rests on~\cite{denning76,sabelfeld03,taintdroid}---a runtime
trusted to see everything---is exactly the one this setting removes, and we show the
audit survives its removal. Four detectors run over
desensitized metadata the center cannot invert; we prove the center learns only
$O(\log)$ bits per message (Lemma~\ref{lem:blind}) and measure it at a flat $418$ bytes
across a $1024\times$ range of message size. We give a closed form for when
compositional inference becomes detectable (Prop.~\ref{prop:k}) and prove attestation
soundness (Prop.~\ref{prop:attest}). Against an adversary that knows the detector
exactly, $20{,}000$ randomized evasion strategies produce none that teaches the
recipient more than the policy permits; ablating either integrity mechanism breaks
that, which is how we know both are load-bearing.
\item \textbf{The privacy typing that makes the leak expressible.} \ext{} separates
the three roles a single identifier conflates today---data subject, owning principal,
agent principal---and carries them in \texttt{Part.metadata}. It composes with
unmodified A2A, and it is the mechanism the two results above stand on: without it
there is nothing for a content-blind center to reason over.
\item \textbf{A2A-MT, calibrated against ground truth we do not control.} The first
multi-tenant agent-privacy benchmark, validated against the external AgentLeak suite
and a properly configured Presidio baseline. Because grading our own detector with our
own tagger would be circular, the measurement above is scored against literal ground
truth taken from the source records; by that standard our own recall is $0.75$, so the
rates we report are lower bounds rather than estimates. Throughout, we bound our own
numbers from both sides---the auditor's recall from below, the attribute ground
truth from above, the tagger from an oracle ceiling---so a reader knows which way
each one can be wrong.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent One implementation serves both the research setting and a deployable
product: set every principal equal and the multi-tenant model collapses to the
\textbf{in-container} case, one organization auditing its own multi-agent app,
with the same detectors and the same zero content egress (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}).

\section{Threat model}\label{sec:threat}
An A2A interaction is a sequence of \texttt{Message}s, each carrying
\texttt{Part}s (text, data, file) and free-form \texttt{metadata}. Nothing in that
model says who a \texttt{Part} is about or who may hold it, so the governance
question has to be posed before it can be answered.

\paragraph{Principals.} A single identifier must not conflate three roles: the
\textbf{data subject} (whom a Part is about), the \textbf{owning principal} (who
controls the datum and the originating agent), and the \textbf{agent principal} (an
agent's AgentCard identity). Governance is defined over principals, not agents: a
datum owned by $P$ may reach an agent of $Q$ only if $Q$ is an allowed recipient for
$P$'s stated purpose.

\paragraph{Adversary.} We consider a receiving or forwarding agent of a different
tenant with three capabilities: (A1) it reads every Part routed to it and may
\emph{infer} beyond what is stated; (A2) it may \emph{paraphrase, split, or re-route}
what it forwards; and (A3) a \emph{dishonest sender} may mislabel its own Parts
(suppress tags or forge a subject id), since labels are produced locally. The
adversary controls neither the A2A transport nor other tenants' processes.

\paragraph{Trust assumptions (software baseline).} (T1) \emph{center-blind}: the
auditor detects from desensitized metadata and never sees raw \texttt{Part} content,
and no tenant reveals content to it or to peers; (T2) \emph{honest-but-curious peers}
for the disclosure, purpose, and TTL detectors---agents route truthfully but try to
learn more than authorized; (T3) \emph{A2A transport is given}---we annotate and
audit, not replace. Under T1--T3 the design defeats A1 and A2 (wording and routing
attacks) by construction, because the detectors key on labels and principals, not on
text (\S\ref{sec:eval}); A3 is the residual the baseline does not close.

\paragraph{Closing A3 with forced-embed attestation.} We remove the honest-labeler
assumption with a downloaded, build-pinned component. The labeler and auditor ship as
a fixed build $B$ whose signing key $k_B$ is confined to $B$; a report is
$R=(\mathcal{E},\sigma)$ with $\sigma=\mathrm{Sign}_{k_B}(h(\mathcal{E}))$ over the
center-view edge set $\mathcal{E}$, and the center accepts $R$ only if it verifies
under $B$'s published fingerprint.

\begin{proposition}[Attestation soundness]\label{prop:attest}
If the signature scheme is \textup{EUF-CMA}-secure and $k_B$ is confined to $B$, then
every report the center accepts equals $B$'s output on the sender's actual inputs,
except with negligible probability. Hence a modified labeler that suppresses a tag or
forges a subject id yields a report signed by an untrusted build (wrong fingerprint)
and is rejected, and content tampering changes $h(\mathcal{E})$ and fails
verification. The residual adversary is confined to (i) exfiltrating $k_B$ from
$B$---the case a hardware root of trust (TEE) closes---and (ii) \emph{sub-threshold}
leakage: disclosing strictly less than the detection threshold, which no
metadata-only auditor can observe because nothing crosses to observe.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Acceptance requires a valid $\sigma$ under $B$'s fingerprint. Producing one for an
$\mathcal{E}$ that $B$ did not emit requires forging a signature (contradicting
EUF-CMA) or obtaining $k_B$ (excluded unless $B$ is broken). Under-tagging and forgery
both require running a labeler other than $B$, whose reports carry no valid $\sigma$;
tampering after signing changes $h(\mathcal{E})$. What remains is not a detection gap
but an information gap: a sender that never emits the datum, or emits fewer than
$k^{*}$ converging fragments (Prop.~\ref{prop:k}), leaves nothing the center could key
on. Canonical owner-derived subject ids (\texttt{canonical\_subject}) make aliasing
impossible without abandoning the attested derivation, collapsing A3's forgery variant
into case~(i).
\end{proof}

\noindent This is the guarantee the evasion study (\S\ref{sec:eval},
Table~\ref{tab:evasion}) exercises empirically.

\section{The \ext{} extension}\label{sec:ext}
The three roles of the threat model become fields the protocol can carry. Each
privacy-relevant \texttt{Part} carries a label stored under the
\texttt{"a2a.privacy/v1"} key of \texttt{Part.metadata}: a \textsf{PrivacyLabel}
with fields \texttt{subject}, \texttt{owner}, \texttt{sensitivity}${\in}[0,5]$,
\texttt{category[]}, \texttt{inferred\_categories[]}, \texttt{purpose[]},
\texttt{allowed\_recipients[]}, \texttt{ttl\_hops}, and \texttt{provenance\_id}.
Identifiers are opaque (\texttt{subject:alice}, \texttt{tenant:hospital})---governance
metadata, never content. An \texttt{AgentClearance} (an AgentCard declaration)
states the purposes an agent is cleared for, so cross-tenant rules are checkable
from public metadata. Two fields are central to the results:
\textbf{inferred\_categories} is computed \emph{locally} (the local auditor sees
content; ``near the oncology center'' $\rightarrow$ \texttt{health}) and only the
tag travels---the signal the inference detector accumulates without the center
reading content; \textbf{provenance\_id} is a stable datum identity assigned by the
origin and preserved by forwarders even when they re-word the data, so hop/TTL
tracking follows a datum across paraphrasing (a content hash breaks the moment a
relay rephrases).

\section{The center-blind auditor and detectors}\label{sec:auditor}
These labels are exactly what the auditor reads, and the content is exactly what
it does not. The auditor desensitizes each labeled \texttt{Part} into a
center-view edge carrying a one-way content hash, the principals, and the
label---no raw text (Figure~\ref{fig:desens}). It
asserts this invariant on its own output: a content token may appear in the center
view only if it is a declared label value or a schema field name; any other token
is a leak.

\begin{lemma}[Bounded content leakage]\label{lem:blind}
Let a message $M$ (with content entropy $H(M)$) produce center-view
$V=(h(M),\ell)$, where $h$ is a hash modeled as a random oracle and
$\ell\in\mathcal{L}$ is the categorical label (category, sensitivity, taint tags,
opaque principal/subject ids). Then the center's information about $M$ satisfies
$I(M;V)\le \log_2|\mathcal{L}|$, a constant independent of $|M|$. Hence for
$H(M)\gg\log_2|\mathcal{L}|$ the center cannot reconstruct $M$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$I(M;V)=I(M;h(M))+I(M;\ell\mid h(M))$. Under the random-oracle model $h(M)$ is a
uniformly random value revealing nothing of $M$ beyond equality tests, so
$I(M;h(M))=0$. The second term is at most $H(\ell)\le\log_2|\mathcal{L}|$. With
$|\mathcal{L}|$ the product of the (small) label domains --- e.g.\ a handful of
categories $\times$ six sensitivity levels --- this is $O(\log)$ bits per Part,
independent of $|M|$; reconstructing $M$ requires $H(M)$ bits, so it is infeasible.
\end{proof}

This is the formal content of ``center-blind'': the auditor operates on a metadata
graph carrying $O(\log)$ bits per message, not a transcript. Four detectors run
over this graph:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cross-tenant disclosure}: sensitive data ($\geq\tau$) reaches a
principal that neither owns it nor is an allowed recipient.
\item \textbf{Purpose limitation}: data reaches an agent whose clearance purposes
are disjoint from the data's permitted purposes.
\item \textbf{Hop/TTL}: a datum (tracked by \texttt{provenance\_id}) is relayed
more than \texttt{ttl\_hops} times; robust to a forwarder re-wording content.
\item \textbf{Cross-tenant inference}: no single edge is a disclosure, but a
recipient principal accumulates converging \texttt{inferred\_categories}
fragments about one subject, letting it infer a sensitive category it was never
authorized for (Figure~\ref{fig:desens}); we ground this in the bound below.
\end{enumerate}


\paragraph{A formal inference-gain bound.} We model what a recipient infers about
a subject's sensitive attribute $A\in\{0,1\}$ from $k$ converging fragments
$f_1,\dots,f_k$, with prior $p_0=P(A{=}1)$ and odds $O_0=p_0/(1-p_0)$.

\begin{assumption}[Conditional independence]\label{as:ci}
Given $A$, the fragments are independent, each with likelihood ratio
$\lambda_i=\tfrac{P(f_i\mid A{=}1)}{P(f_i\mid A{=}0)}\ge1$; write $\lambda$ for a
common lower bound $\lambda_i\ge\lambda>1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:k}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:ci} the posterior odds are $O_k=O_0\prod_i\lambda_i$, the
posterior is $P(A\mid f_{1:k})=O_k/(1+O_k)$, and the inference gain
$g(k)=P(A\mid f_{1:k})-p_0$ is strictly increasing in $k$. With uniform $\lambda$,
the detector (fires iff $g(k)\ge\delta$) fires exactly for
$k\ge k^{*}=\big\lceil\log_\lambda(O_\delta/O_0)\big\rceil$, where
$O_\delta=(p_0+\delta)/(1-p_0-\delta)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Bayes in odds form, $O_k=O_0\prod_i\lambda_i$; since each $\lambda_i>1$, $O_k$
is strictly increasing in $k$, and $x\mapsto x/(1+x)$ is strictly increasing, so
$g$ is strictly increasing. With uniform $\lambda$, $g(k)\ge\delta \iff
P(A\mid f_{1:k})\ge p_0+\delta \iff O_k\ge O_\delta \iff O_0\lambda^{k}\ge O_\delta
\iff k\ge\log_\lambda(O_\delta/O_0)$; integrality gives the ceiling.
\end{proof}
For defaults $(p_0,\lambda,\delta)=(0.1,3,0.3)$, $k^{*}=2$: one hint never fires,
two converging fragments do (posterior $0.50$, gain $0.40$), replacing the
heuristic $1-2^{-k}$ with a calibrated quantity and a closed-form bound (Figure~\ref{fig:gain});
deployments tune $(p_0,\lambda,\delta)$ and may set $\lambda_i$ from the tagger's
hint specificity (so one high-$\lambda$ fragment can suffice).
\emph{On the assumption:} real fragments are correlated, so
Assumption~\ref{as:ci} is an approximation --- positively-correlated (redundant)
hints make the true joint ratio smaller than $\prod\lambda_i$, so the model can
over-credit evidence; empirically the detector nonetheless \emph{under}-fires
relative to a real LLM recipient, because the tagger, not the bound, is the
binding constraint. The bound is thus a calibrated design tool, not a tight
guarantee.

\section{The A2A-MT benchmark}
AgentLeak and Sum-Leaks are single-tenant---the vault belongs to one system and
the boundary is system$\rightarrow$output---and no existing suite holds an auditor
to center-blind evidence. A2A-MT supplies labeled multi-tenant A2A interactions
whose boundary is \emph{between} agents and whose ground truth is detectable from
labels and hashes alone. A curated \emph{calendar-negotiation} family (two people's
scheduling agents must reveal availability without leaking \emph{why} a slot is
blocked) instantiates all four violation types; parameterized families
(group-assistant, marketplace-delegation, cross-tenant aggregation, plus
disclosure, purpose, TTL, and inference sweeps with near-miss clean controls) give 48
labeled scenarios, ground truth set by template intent. An LLM fills each Part
with realistic text matching its label (labels remain the ground truth), exported
as a shareable artifact.

The closest existing suite is WeClawArena~\cite{weclaw}, which pairs cross-user
agent collaboration with attack variants spanning collaboration, security, privacy
and governance. It differs from A2A-MT in the assumption that matters here: it is an
\emph{auditable sandbox}, so the harness observes the workspace and can grade from
what it sees. A2A-MT instead holds the auditor to what a mutually-distrusting
deployment would actually expose---labels and hashes---so a scenario is only
``detected'' if it is detectable without reading the message. The two ask different
questions of the same setting and we take the sandbox result as the upper bound our
center-blind numbers sit below.

Its schema is also independent evidence for the typing in \S\ref{sec:ext}. Built
separately and for a different purpose, each of its resources carries an owner
distinct from the agent acting on it, a sensitivity level, protected fields, and
scope-limited operations---three of the four content-governance fields \ext{}
defines, arrived at without reference to this work. The field it does not carry is
the \emph{data subject}: in its clinical scenarios the attending's owner holds the
case file, and the patient the file is about has no representation at all. That
omission is the one the regimes this paper targets turn on, and it is what the
three-way split is for.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
In three parts, in the order the claims depend on each other. First, what agents
actually do at an organizational boundary, and whether anything short of a
structural safeguard changes it. Second, whether an auditor that may not read the
message can nonetheless catch what they do, against the tools a practitioner would
reach for and against an adversary who knows exactly how it decides. Third, every
way we could find to break our own result---an author who is not us, a ground
truth we do not control, and an ablation that bounds what our weakest component
could ever be worth.

\subsection{Agents in the wild}
Away from any labeled benchmark, we measure how real agents behave: 24 realistic
multi-agent workflows across 7 domains, each holding a sensitive record, where a
coordinator agent is asked to delegate to an \emph{external} specialist with no
engineered leaks, run 5$\times$ each (120 trials) for \emph{each} of four recipient
models spanning open-weight (Qwen2.5-7B/14B, Llama-3.1-8B) and frontier
(Claude~Opus~4.8). Over-sharing is prevalent across every model and, revealingly,
\emph{falls with capability but never disappears}: the open models leak the subject's
sensitive identifiers (SSN, MRN, account/card numbers, diagnoses) in \textbf{78--82\%}
of trials, the aligned frontier model in \textbf{46\%} (Table~\ref{tab:wild}). Every
cross-purpose hand-off is a purpose violation by construction, and the center-blind
auditor flagged all of them with \textbf{zero raw content reaching the center on every
model}. This is the deployment-measurement counterpart to AgentLeak on the
multi-tenant setting: left to their own judgment, current agents---weak and strong
alike---routinely over-share a subject's identifiers across organizational boundaries,
and the auditor catches it without seeing the data.

\subsection{Can the agent simply be told not to?}\label{sec:instr}
The measurement above invites an obvious objection: our coordinator prompt ends
\emph{``include whatever you think is helpful''}, so a reader may reasonably
suspect the leak is our wording rather than the model's behavior. It also leaves
this paper's central claim---that the safeguard has to be structural---as an
assertion. We test both by sweeping the instruction alone, over the same 24
workflows, ten models---nine open-weight models from five families and a frontier model---and $2{,}880$ trials. Ground truth here is
deliberately \emph{independent of our auditor}: literal identifiers and attribute
terms taken from the source record, matched against what the coordinator actually
wrote, with each pattern checked against its own record so a drifted annotation
fails loudly rather than scoring zero in our favor.

Removing the invitation changes nothing ($96\%\!\to\!98\%$): the over-sharing is
the models', not the prompt's. Instructing the agent helps, and the shape of the
help is the finding. Naming the forbidden categories explicitly leaves $82\%$; handing the model the
record's actual policy---owner, permitted recipient, purpose, and the fact that
this recipient is \emph{not} permitted---leaves $74\%$, and even the frontier model
under the strongest condition still leaks in $36\%$ of hand-offs
(Table~\ref{tab:instr}).

The breakdown is sharper than the totals. Instructions suppress hard identifiers
markedly more effectively than sensitive attributes ($79\!\to\!38\%$ against $88\!\to\!65\%$): models learn not to paste the account number and go on writing
the diagnosis, a redaction-shaped reflex rather than an understanding of what is
sensitive. Nor is a bigger model the fix: capability moves the identifier line---Llama-3.1-8B
already beats the larger Qwen2.5-14B on it, and the frontier model drives it to
$1\%$---while the attribute line barely follows, so neither a better prompt nor a
better model closes the gap.
Figure~\ref{fig:redaction} shows the split model by model, ordered by size: what grows with scale is the redaction of identifiers, not the protection of attributes. This is the evidence the structural claim needed.

The same experiment costs us a number we would rather not report. Against this
independent ground truth our own auditor's recall is $0.75$ ($639$ misses in $2{,}880$), so the rates in Table~\ref{tab:wild} are \emph{lower bounds} on
over-sharing, not estimates of it. Precision is $0.90$; the 199 firings without a literal match are
largely paraphrased disclosures the literal patterns cannot see. Both measures
are lower bounds on different things and neither dominates the other.

\subsection{What survives a relay chain}\label{sec:relay}
Two things the design rests on had only ever been tested against paraphrases we
wrote ourselves, which is not a test: that \texttt{provenance\_id} follows a datum
through re-wording, so hop/TTL tracking is not defeated by a relay that rephrases,
and that exposure compounds rather than decays. We let real agents do the
rephrasing---each forwards the case onward in its own words, four hops deep,
against a hop budget of two---over the same 24 records, three models, 144 chains.

Exposure does not decay. Two of the three models still carry the subject's
identifiers or attributes at hop~4 in \emph{every} chain ($100\%$); the third
declines only from $94\%$ to $79\%$. The intuition that passing a datum through
successive re-writes dilutes it is simply wrong, and the same identifier/attribute
asymmetry appears again from an entirely different mechanism---Mistral-7B's
identifier rate falls across the chain ($90\!\to\!83\%$) while its attribute rate
\emph{rises} ($94\!\to\!96\%$). Meanwhile the tracking holds: provenance survived
all four model paraphrases in $144/144$ chains and the TTL violation fired in
$144/144$, with zero raw content reaching the center. The failure we were looking
for---a datum that keeps leaking while the auditor loses track of it---did not
occur.

\subsection{Is the inference threat real?}
Our detector fires at $k^{*}=2$ hints, but is that where real inferability begins? We
have an independent LLM play the recipient and infer the subject's withheld attribute
from the $K$ benign fragments it received ($K=1,2,3$), scaled to \textbf{16 sensitive
attributes} across health, finance, employment, legal, and behavioral domains
($5$ trials each). \emph{The threat is real, and for a frontier recipient it begins
below our threshold:} Claude Opus~4.8 infers the withheld attribute from two benign
fragments in \textbf{100\% (16/16)} of attributes and from a \emph{single} fragment in
\textbf{81\% (13/16)}. Weaker recipients need more evidence: on the same 16-attribute
set, open-weight recipients infer at $K{=}2$ in 56--69\% (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B)
and 81\% (Qwen2.5-14B) of attributes, versus 100\% for the frontier model---inference
at the detection threshold rises with capability, so the compositional threat grows as
agents improve. \emph{This makes our detector
a conservative, no-false-alarm lower bound:} it stays silent at $K=1$, yet a strong
recipient already infers there; every disagreement is an under-fire, and we never fire
where the recipient cannot infer. Hence detection must flag the metadata pattern, not
try to out-infer the adversary. The
threshold $k^{*}=2$ with uniform evidence is conservative: real inference sometimes needs one high-specificity hint, which
the Bayesian model of \S\ref{sec:auditor} accommodates via a per-fragment
$\lambda$; wiring
$\lambda$ to hint specificity catches single strong hints without over-firing.

\subsection{Comparison with real baselines}
A hand-rolled regular-expression scanner is not a credible DLP baseline, so we run
\textbf{Microsoft Presidio}~\cite{presidio} itself, in two realistic configurations: a
strict identifier policy (SSN, card, email, phone, bank, passport, licence) and a
broad one that adds \texttt{PERSON}, \texttt{LOCATION}, \texttt{NRP} and
\texttt{DATE\_TIME}. The set is 32 realistic scenarios (17 leaks / 15 clean) spanning
all four violation types plus the cases that separate the approaches: non-regex PII, a
diagnosis, authorized external sharing, benign traffic, and single-hint near-misses
that must \emph{not} fire (Table~\ref{tab:frontier}).

\emph{Structurally}, every baseline must ingest content while ours ingests none---the
disqualifier for regulated, cross-tenant deployments, independent of accuracy.
\emph{On accuracy}, no Presidio configuration wins on both axes. The identifier policy
reaches only $F1{=}0.26$: it has no notion of owner, purpose, or hop budget, so it
misses inference, purpose, and TTL violations entirely, and the three clean scenarios
it does flag are precisely the \emph{authorized} external shares. The broad policy
buys recall ($0.65$) only by firing indiscriminately---specificity falls to $0.47$,
flagging eight of fifteen clean scenarios including a lunch invitation and a slide
deck---so its apparently strong $83\%$ ``inference recall'' is not inference detection
at all, merely the observation that those messages contain a place and a date. A
policy-aware LLM-judge that reads every byte is precise but insensitive
($F1{=}0.58$, $0\%$ inference recall). Ours leads at $F1{=}0.94$ with specificity
$1.00$ and \emph{zero} content reaching the center; every miss is tagger coverage
(employment- and behavioral-domain hints), never a false alarm. Consistent with the
tagger study below, the open-weight LLM tagger does not help here---it slightly
\emph{trails} the lexical floor---so the lift we previously measured was specific to a
proprietary backend.

\paragraph{A shipped guardrail.} Presidio is a pattern matcher and the LLM-judge is

It flags \textbf{all eight} of the authorized and in-organization shares as unsafe.
Those carry exactly the same sensitive content as the leaks; the only difference is
that the recipient is entitled to it. And it calls \textbf{all six} compositional
inference cases safe, because no single message in them contains a hazard.

Both failures are architectural rather than a matter of capacity. The hazard
taxonomy has no slot for \emph{who may receive} a datum, so an authorized share and
a leak are the same message to it; and it judges messages one at a time, so
evidence that only becomes disclosive when it accumulates is invisible. A larger
Llama Guard would share both blind spots exactly --- which is why we report the 1B
variant, forced by local disk, without claiming it is the strongest available: the
limit being demonstrated is not one that scale removes.

\subsection{Adaptive evasion: what can an adversary learn while evading?}
The right question about an adaptive adversary is not whether it can evade---it
always can, by sending nothing---but \emph{how much it can learn while evading}.
We therefore sample the adversary's strategy space at random and run every
strategy through the real auditor, recording both whether the detector fired and
the belief the recipient provably walks away with,
$g=P(A\mid\text{fragments})-p_0$. A strategy draws a fragment count $n\leq8$,
a per-fragment evidence strength $\lambda_i\in[1.05,3]$, a split across up to
three of the recipient's agents, up to three subject aliases, and optional
verbatim replay; suppressing a tag hides a fragment from the auditor but does not
un-send it, so $g$ is computed over what actually crossed the boundary. Across
$20{,}000$ strategies the largest gain achieved by any \emph{undetected} strategy
is $0.29992$ against the policy threshold $\delta{=}0.3$: not one evasion crossed
the line (Table~\ref{tab:evasion}). Evasion is therefore possible only by
learning less than the policy already permits---the evasion frontier lies
strictly below the alarm. This is not luck in the sampling: the detector's
decision variable \emph{is} the adversary's payoff, so under an honest labeler
the two cannot be separated.

The two integrity mechanisms are load-bearing rather than decorative, and
ablating them says by how much. Removing forced-embed attestation lets the sender
run a labeler that suppresses its own inference tags; sweeping the tag rate $q$,
detection falls from $0.54$ to $0$ as $q\to0$ and $53.6\%$ of strategies evade
\emph{while} crossing $\delta$---leaking at full strength, unseen. Removing
canonical owner-derived subject ids lets the sender alias the subject and break
the grouping the detector accumulates over: $16.3\%$ become harmful evasions. No
configuration ever put raw content in the center view. We \emph{implement} both
defenses:
the auditor+tagger ship as a build-pinned component whose key is held only by the
unmodified binary, and the center accepts a report only from a trusted build
fingerprint. Under-tagging and forgery require modifying the labeler, so the
report is signed by an untrusted build and is \textbf{rejected}; tampering is
caught by an edges-hash mismatch; canonical owner-derived subject ids
(\texttt{canonical\_subject}) make aliasing impossible without abandoning the
attested derivation. The evasions become detected non-compliance, leaving only
key compromise (the TEE regime) as residual---the guarantee stated formally in
Prop.~\ref{prop:attest} (\S\ref{sec:threat}). Partial deployment degrades
gracefully but not for free: a center that requires attestation rejects an
unattested report outright, so the adversary can only hide on an unenrolled
tenant, and at enrollment coverage $c$ the residual harmful-evasion rate is
$(1-c)\times53.6\%$---$c{=}0.99$ to hold it below $1\%$.

\paragraph{Attacking the tagger instead.} The study above assumes honest tags;
the tagger is the one component that reads content, so we attack it directly. An
attacking model is given the protected fact and, in the white-box arm, the lexical
tagger's entire 141-term lexicon with the instruction to avoid every term. A
hand-off \emph{evades} only if a reader model from a different family, shown the
hand-off alone, judges the fact conveyed \emph{and} the auditor fires no
content-derived violation---saying nothing is not evasion. Across five attackers (7B to 32B) and 480 hand-offs, the lexical tagger is evaded $55\%$ of the time black-box and $66\%$ white-box; against the 32B attacker it catches none of 48
white-box hand-offs. The LLM tagger, facing the same hand-offs, is evaded $2\%$ $[0,5]$ black-box and $8\%$ $[2,14]$ white-box (cluster bootstrap over facts), and
black-box evasion does not rise with attacker strength; swapping in a smaller
tagger from a third family (8B) raises evasion to $8\%$ and $12\%$, so the figure
tracks tagger strength rather than a shared lineage. Avoiding the vocabulary
also costs the attacker the message: conveyance falls from $87\%$ to $82\%$. This
is the thesis on the defender's side---a token list fails against any sender able
to follow ``avoid these words,'' while a tagger that judges meaning does not. Query access is the stronger attack, and we ran it: handed the LLM tagger's verdict and three rewrites, two attackers raise evasion to $16\%$ $[7,26]$---and lower conveyance to $60\%$, because what passes the tagger increasingly fails to say the thing. The tagger is not unbeatable; it is expensive to beat, and the price is the message.

\subsection{What the audit costs, and what actually crosses}\label{sec:cost}
Lemma~\ref{lem:blind} says the center's view of a message is a hash plus a
categorical label, so its size cannot depend on the message. That is a claim about
the implementation as much as the mathematics, and it is falsifiable: we sweep
content length over three orders of magnitude and measure the bytes that actually
cross. They are constant at \textbf{418 bytes per message} from 64-byte to
64\,KB messages---$0.0\%$ variation across a $1024\times$ range. A flat line is the
lemma; any slope would have been message length leaking into the center view.

Throughput is $32$--$40$k messages/s single-process, and per-message cost grows
only $1.24\times$ from $100$ to $50{,}000$ messages: the detectors are linear in
edges, so an audit scales with traffic rather than with history. We state the
comparison against a content-shipping observer as a crossover rather than a win,
because it is one. At $16$\,KB messages the center receives $39\times$ fewer bytes;
at 64-byte messages it receives $6.5\times$ \emph{more}, since 418 bytes of metadata
then outweighs the message it describes. Lemma~\ref{lem:blind} bounds what the
center \emph{learns}, not what it receives---the guarantee is that the view cannot
be inverted to content and does not grow with it, not that it is always smaller.

\subsection{The self-authored floor}
On all 48 A2A-MT scenarios the auditor scores $P/R/F1=1.0$ (TP$=18$, FP$=0$,
TN$=30$, FN$=0$) with full per-type coverage and no over-firing on single-hint
clean controls, and holds $1.0$ when the templated text is replaced by
LLM-generated prose---the detectors key on labels, not wording. We report this
first and weight it least: we wrote both the detectors and the scenarios, so it
measures the detectors \emph{given correct labels} and nothing more. Everything
that follows is an attempt to break it. Generating the realistic variant did earn
its keep in one way hand-written tests could not: it exposed TTL fragility under
paraphrasing (fixed by \texttt{provenance\_id}) and an invariant false positive on
prose tokens coinciding with label tokens.

\subsection{The local tagger}
The inference
detector accumulates \texttt{inferred\_categories}; in a real deployment these
must be \emph{produced} from content, not hand-set. A local tagger (lexical,
zero-dependency, optional LLM backend) emits only tags---content never leaves the
agent. On a labeled set with paraphrased cases, the lexical backend recovers explicit
(sensitive) \texttt{category} at $P/R/F1=1.0$ and \texttt{inferred\_categories}
at $P=1.0$, $R=0.78$; the \textbf{LLM backend (gpt-4o-mini) lifts inferred-category
recall $0.78\to1.0$} (the misses are novel paraphrases like ``the place where they
treat tumors'').
That lift, however, is \emph{backend-dependent}, and we report the negative result:
on the same labeled set, open-weight backends served locally (Qwen2.5-7B/14B,
Llama-3.1-8B) do \emph{not} clear the paraphrase gap---each sits at or below the
lexical floor's $R{=}0.78$, and Llama-3.1-8B is materially worse ($R{=}0.56$)---while
also \emph{costing} explicit-category precision ($1.0\to0.38$--$0.75$) by over-tagging
benign text. Qwen2.5-14B is the partial exception, raising inferred \emph{precision}
to $0.88$ (best inferred $F1{=}0.82$) with recall still at $0.78$. Capability, not
merely ``being an LLM'', is what closes the gap: a deployer cannot assume that
swapping in any local model buys inference coverage.
\texttt{AuditSession.observe} wires it in so a deployer supplies only text plus
policy. Two different things limit the tagger and only one of them is closed by
attestation: a \emph{dishonest} sender that suppresses tags is caught because its
report is not signed by the pinned build, but an \emph{honest} tagger that simply
cannot name a category leaves a gap no signature can see. The latter is the recall
ceiling measured below.

\subsection{An author-independent benchmark}
Our 48-scenario benchmark scores 1.0, but we wrote both the detectors and the
scenarios. To de-bias it, an LLM (\emph{not} the detector author) generates diverse
scenarios with its own intended labels and we score our auditor against them. Pooling
six batches gives \textbf{75 scenarios} (46 intended leaks), every one of which parses
and is scored---no silent sample shrinkage. The honest result sits far below the
self-authored 1.0: with the lexical tagger $P{=}1.00$, $R{=}0.17$; with an open-weight
LLM tagger recall rises to $R{=}0.46$ but precision falls to $P{=}0.88$ (three false
positives). That last number sharpens a claim we would otherwise have overstated: the
architecture does not over-fire \emph{given correct labels}, but an over-tagging
backend can still manufacture false positives end-to-end, so perfect precision is a
property of the auditor, not a guarantee of the deployed pipeline.

\paragraph{Where the recall actually goes.} It would be easy to attribute this gap
to taxonomy coverage and move on; we tested the attribution instead, on a fresh pool
and with the LLM tagger moved to a different model family (Mistral-7B) than the
author, so that one model is not both writing and grading the scenarios---which is
why its absolute numbers differ from the pool above. Scoring one
pool of $99$ author-generated scenarios under progressively more generous taggers
bounds what any tagger fix can buy (Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}), and the result
corrects us in two places.

First, \textbf{the ceiling is $0.76$, not $1.0$}. Even with every part marked
maximally sensitive and carrying every inferred category, roughly a quarter of the
author's intended leaks go undetected. That remainder is not reachable by any
tagger: it is detector scope plus the author's own label noise, and no amount of
taxonomy work will move it. Second, within what \emph{is} reachable, the binding
constraint is the \textbf{sensitivity estimate}, not category coverage---conceding
sensitivity alone recovers the entire reachable gap, while conceding every inferred
category recovers \emph{nothing} ($R{=}0.22$, identical to the lexical floor to the
last confusion-matrix cell). The reason is structural rather than lexical: the
author writes predominantly single-hop scenarios, so converging fragments never
reach $k^{*}{=}2$ and the inference detector cannot contribute at all. Our earlier
reading of the miss taxonomy---that the misses split evenly between disclosure and
inference---was measuring the author's scenario mix, not our detector.

Taxonomy coverage is real but secondary: letting the tagger name the sensitive kind
in open vocabulary instead of choosing from our ten classes lifts recall
$0.37\to0.43$. The oracles are upper bounds and not configurations---flagging
everything costs precision ($0.85\to0.74$)---but they say clearly which component
is worth improving, and that the honest ceiling for this pool is $0.76$ rather than
the $1.0$ a reader would otherwise assume.

\subsection{External validation on AgentLeak}
The multi-tenant
auditor reuses a federated engine we validate on the external \textbf{AgentLeak}
benchmark. Driving AgentLeak's own 600 multi-agent scenarios through an LLM to
produce real inter-agent traces and labeling leakage by AgentLeak's rule, the
auditor detects \textbf{100\% of internal-channel leaks at 0.972 precision with
zero raw vault content reaching the center} ($82.5\%$ internal-channel leak rate,
matching AgentLeak). On a labeled 33-scenario set the engine holds $P/R/F1=1.0$,
and under the full six-layer desensitizer plus DP it holds $F1\approx0.97$ at
$\varepsilon\in[0.5,3]$ with zero raw leakage, the privacy--utility result the
multi-tenant typing builds on.

\subsection{Is the operating point cherry-picked?}
The inference detector fires at $k^{*}{=}2$ under our defaults, so it is fair to ask
whether that is a knife-edge fitted to the benchmark. We sweep each knob over the full
48-scenario suite (deterministic; no LLM calls) and find a broad plateau
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}). \emph{Every} setting of $(p_0,\lambda,\delta)$ for which the
closed form gives $k^{*}{=}2$ scores $P/R/F1=1.0$---roughly a $2\times$ band in each
knob independently---and quality degrades only once $k^{*}$ moves off $2$, in the
direction the model predicts: $k^{*}{=}1$ over-fires ($P{=}0.86$) and $k^{*}\ge3$
under-fires ($R{=}0.83$). Detection is therefore a function of the closed-form
$k^{*}$ rather than of the raw parameter values, so the three-parameter model
collapses to a single effective knob and the calibration is principled rather than
tuned. The disclosure floor $\tau$ degrades gracefully rather than cliff-edging
($\tau{=}2$: $P{=}0.90$; $\tau{=}4$: $R{=}0.94$; $\tau{=}5$: $R{=}0.89$), and
raw$\to$center $=0$ at every setting.

\subsection{Metadata privacy under differential privacy}
Beyond hiding content, we harden the
metadata: identity-bearing fields are pseudonymized with a per-audit shared salt
(cross-tenant comparisons hold in pseudonym space; the center learns no real
identity) and \texttt{sensitivity} is DP-noised, while categories are kept
structurally. Pseudonymization alone is lossless ($P/R/F1=1.0$). Under DP we
sweep two orders of magnitude, $\varepsilon\in[0.1,8]$, at 40 trials each: mean
$F1$ decays monotonically and gracefully---$0.99$ at $\varepsilon{=}3$, $0.98$ at
$1$, $0.96$ at $0.5$, and still $\approx0.93$ at $0.1$ (sd $\le0.033$)---with no
cliff. The loss is concentrated in recall, i.e.\ disclosure decisions whose noised
sensitivity crosses the floor, exactly where the mechanism predicts, and zero raw
content reaches the center at every $\varepsilon$.

\section{Discussion}
Center-blind detection works for a narrow reason worth stating plainly: the risks
it targets happen to be visible in the metadata graph---principals, categories,
inferred-category tags, provenance---all of which the local side can emit without
revealing content. That is a property of these risks, not a general result. A
harm that lives only in the wording of a message, with no governance correlate,
would be invisible to this design, and we would not know.

\input{figures/fig_trends}

The two trends in Figure~\ref{fig:trends} are why we think the safeguard cannot be
behavioral, and they are worth reading as one statement rather than two. Explicit
over-sharing falls with capability while compositional inference rises with it, so
there is no point on the capability axis at which trusting the recipient is
reasonable: the weak model pastes the identifier outright, and the strong one does
not need it. Aligning models better moves a defender along this curve; it does not
get them off it. The auditor keys on the metadata pattern instead---a sensitive tag
crossing a boundary, or converging inferred-category fragments---and so is
indifferent to which end of the curve a deployment happens to sit on. That is the
same observation as the instruction result, seen from the defender's side: the
leak was never in the tokens, so neither redaction nor pattern-matching could
remove it, and only a detector that reasons about what the recipient can now
infer has anything to key on.

\paragraph{Literal compliance.} The instruction result is, read one way, an
instruction-following success: every model did more of what it was told as the
telling got more explicit, and the frontier model did it almost perfectly. The
failure is in what the instruction could express. ``Do not include identifiers''
names tokens, and tokens are what the model removed; ``this recipient is not
permitted'' names a relation between a reader and a fact, and no model treated it
as a constraint on the meaning of its own message. Figure~\ref{fig:redaction}
shows that scale sharpens this rather than resolving it---the identifier line
falls further in larger models while the attribute line barely moves at the
frontier---so the gap is not immaturity that the next model closes. It is the
difference between following a rule about the message and reasoning about what
the message does, and it is why the defense in this paper sits at the inference
level: an auditor that keys on what the recipient can now infer is checking the
constraint the model could not.

\paragraph{What we have not established.} Five limits, in the order we would
attack them.

\emph{The roster.} Ten models, one of them frontier. The frontier arm did
partly overturn our first phrasing and we have changed it: ``instructions do not
work'' is false as stated---they work very well on identifiers---and what survives
is the narrower, sharper claim that they buy redaction rather than privacy. We have
one frontier model, not a family of them, so whether that split is a property of
alignment training in general or of this model is open.

\emph{The ceiling.} Recall on author-independent scenarios is low, and the oracle
ablation says the fix is narrower than we first claimed: about a quarter of the
author's intended leaks are beyond \emph{any} tagger, and the reachable part is
bounded by the sensitivity estimate rather than by taxonomy coverage.

\emph{The pipeline, not the auditor.} The auditor does not over-fire given correct
labels, but that is a property of the auditor and not of a deployment: a noisy
tagger still costs precision end to end. Relatedly, the inference bound assumes
conditionally independent fragments, which real, correlated hints only approximate.

\emph{This is a detector, not a gate.} Everything measured here is detection: the
auditor says a hand-off violated the policy, after the fact and from metadata. It
does not sit in the path and stop one. Whether the same signal can drive
enforcement---block, hand the agent the reason, let it rewrite---is a separate
question with its own cost in task completion, and we do not claim it here.

\emph{The attribute ground truth over-calls, and we can say by how much.} Three
independent model judges labeled a blinded sample of 100 hand-offs---none of them
the model that wrote our patterns, none judging its own output. On identifiers our
patterns are balanced against the judges' majority ($74\%$ against $76\%$, erring
in both directions about equally). On \emph{attributes} they fire more often than
the majority does, $83\%$ against $72\%$: we call a leak the judges do not in
$16\%$ of sampled hand-offs, against $5\%$ the other way.

Adjudicating those rows gives a characterizable cause, and it is the failure mode
we had already found and thought we had removed: a pattern naming the \emph{task}
rather than the secret. A car dispatcher has to be told the passenger travels with
a security detail; an e-discovery vendor is routinely told a memo is privileged;
and a request for \emph{anonymized} disciplinary trends does not disclose the
student. Our patterns fired on all three. We have not re-tightened them to match
the judges---doing so would make model judges the ground truth, and the weakest of
them marks $39$ attribute positives where the others mark $75$ and $82$---so
attribute-derived rates here should be read as \emph{upper} bounds on the attribute
half, exactly as the auditor's detections are lower bounds on the whole. Human
adjudication is outstanding and would settle it.

\emph{The residual adversary.} Two remain, and they are not equally serious. A peer
that keeps every recipient below the convergence threshold is real but bounded---by
construction it learns less than the policy already permits, which is what the
evasion frontier measures. A colluding sender--recipient pair that suppresses a
shared datum is the genuine residual, and only a hardware root of trust (the TEE
backend our attestation admits) closes it.

\section{Related work}

\noindent\textbf{Agent auditing.} Nian et al.~\cite{auditableagents} frame
auditability for agent systems as evidence that makes accountability possible, and
separate the mechanism classes that produce it---detect, enforce, recover. This
paper is a \emph{detect} mechanism operating under an unusual restriction: the
detecting party may not read the evidence. The enforcement counterpart is a
pre-execution firewall such as AEGIS~\cite{aegis}, which interposes on the
tool-execution path, scans the arguments, and hash-chains its decisions into a
tamper-evident trail. That design is complementary and assumes the opposite of
ours: it sits inside one party's runtime and inspects the very content our center
is forbidden to see. The two compose---per-tenant enforcement locally, center-blind
detection across the boundary---but neither substitutes for the other.

\noindent\textbf{Privacy leakage in LLM-agent systems.} AgentLeak~\cite{agentleak}
and Sum-Leaks~\cite{sumleaks} measure and mitigate \emph{single-tenant}
compositional leakage; privacy-theory work for agents~\cite{dpagents,itlimits}
analyzes when local privacy fails to compose. LLMs also memorize and regurgitate
training data~\cite{carlini21,carlini23}. We lift the setting to \emph{multi-tenant}
and supply a deployable, center-blind detector rather than a measurement or a
theory.

\noindent\textbf{Agent communication protocols.} A2A~\cite{a2a}, MCP~\cite{mcp},
and related standards define agent interoperability but no data governance;
comparative security analyses~\cite{protosec,protoexploit} flag exactly this gap,
which we fill inside A2A's extension mechanism.

\noindent\textbf{Information-flow control and taint tracking.} Our detectors are a
lightweight, semantic information-flow analysis: the lattice model~\cite{denning76}
and language-based IFC~\cite{sabelfeld03} formalize where data may flow, and
dynamic taint tracking~\cite{taintdroid} enforces it at runtime. We adapt these to
opaque LLM agents over metadata (not program variables), adding purpose and
cross-tenant semantics and \emph{compositional inference}, which classical IFC
does not model. The sharper difference is in what each assumes it may read: IFC
enforcement presumes a runtime trusted to inspect every labeled value, which is
exactly the party that cannot exist between mutually-distrusting tenants. Our
detectors are therefore constrained to decide from labels alone, and the
$O(\log)$-bit bound (Lemma~\ref{lem:blind}) is what makes that constraint a
guarantee rather than a limitation. Purpose- and recipient-limitation follow the norm of contextual
integrity~\cite{ci}.

\noindent\textbf{Data-loss prevention.} DLP / PII scanners (e.g.\
Presidio~\cite{presidio}) match sensitive patterns in content. We run Presidio
itself as a baseline (\S\ref{sec:eval}): it sees raw data, has no policy or purpose
semantics, and no configuration of it wins on both axes---its identifier policy is
insensitive to inference, purpose and hop violations, while a broad policy becomes
indiscriminate. We are center-blind and compositional.

\noindent\textbf{Anonymization and differential privacy.} $k$-anonymity~\cite{ksweeney}
and $\ell$-diversity~\cite{ldiv} motivate our structural domain generalization;
differential privacy~\cite{dwork06,dworkroth} and local DP~\cite{rappor} underlie
our metadata hardening (\S\ref{sec:eval}). Unlike these, our object is \emph{detection} of a
composed leak, not release of a sanitized dataset.

\noindent\textbf{Federated and secure computation.} The federated split follows
federated learning's keep-data-local principle~\cite{fedavg}; stronger guarantees
are available via secure computation~\cite{yao} and trusted execution, which our
attestation layer (\S\ref{sec:eval}) plugs into. We target a deployable software baseline with a
TEE upgrade path.

\noindent\textbf{Observability.} LangSmith and Langfuse~\cite{langfuse} trace
agent runs but must ingest raw prompts and outputs, the disqualifier for
regulated, cross-tenant deployments and the one property our design structurally
avoids.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
This work involves no human subjects and required no IRB review. Every scenario is
synthetic: the A2A-MT benchmark and the in-the-wild study use LLM-generated content
populated with fictional identifiers (invented names and format-valid but
non-real SSNs, MRNs, and account numbers), and the external validation runs on the
already-public AgentLeak benchmark. No real personal data is collected, stored, or
transmitted. The artifact is defensive by construction and center-blind in
operation: it detects privacy violations from desensitized metadata and, by design,
no raw content reaches the central component even in deployment. We considered
dual-use: our threat model and inference detector describe how compositional
leakage arises, but that same analysis is what a defender needs, and we disclose no
attack beyond what AgentLeak and Sum-Leaks already document. No component met the
threshold for an external ethics panel; we are glad to clarify any concern with the
program committee.

\section*{Open Science}
The full system---the \ext{} extension, the center-blind auditor and its four
detectors, the local tagger, the attestation layer---and the A2A-MT benchmark
generator are implemented and will be released under an open-source license upon
publication, together with scripts that reproduce every quantitative result in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}. The repository is public today under the authors' names, so to preserve double-blind review we withhold the link rather than publish a scrubbed copy; it is released with the camera-ready, and every number in this paper can be regenerated from the versioned traces it contains. The benchmark is exported as a versioned, shareable artifact so results are
reproducible independently of our implementation, and we intend to submit it for
artifact evaluation.
